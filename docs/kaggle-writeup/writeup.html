Our RSNA Knee MRI Solution: What Worked, What Failed, and What We Learned

2.5D readers, weak labels, a four-model ensemble, and a public-weights comparison

Thanks to RSNA, Kaggle, the contributing institutions and the authors who shared code, models and ideas. This is a retrospective write-up of our project. We did not win a medal, but the experiments produced useful lessons about supervision, model selection and working within a small compute budget.

1. The problem: images at test time, reports during training

The task was to predict twelve knee MRI findings. There were 4,407 development studies, 24,371 series and expert labels for only 58 studies. Most training supervision therefore came from radiology reports. At inference, the model had to work from the MRI alone.

That distinction mattered. A report can omit a finding or describe something that does not satisfy the competition's positive threshold. A model that gets better at reproducing report labels may not get equally better at reading images. We used report-derived validation extensively, and we keep those scores separate from organizer-evaluated leaderboard scores throughout this write-up.

2. Our image pipeline

Figure 1. Our independently trained reader and Ens4. This is a schematic, with no patient images.

The core idea was simple: treat one study as a bag of small image windows.

  1. Sort DICOM slices using their physical geometry.
  2. Canonicalize orientation and create a 160-mm central field of view.
  3. Cache 256-pixel uint8 volumes using foreground percentile scaling.
  4. Select sagittal, coronal and axial series, preferring fluid-sensitive and fat-suppressed inputs.
  5. Stack three adjacent slices from one series into a three-channel 2.5D window.
  6. Encode valid windows with one shared 2D CNN.
  7. Pool the window features and produce twelve study-level outputs.

Most training recipes sampled four windows per plane; inference used sixteen evenly spaced windows per plane. Inputs were resized to 224 pixels and used ImageNet normalization. Missing windows were masked. Laterality-aware mirroring was applied in the loading/inference path, rather than assuming an arbitrary horizontal flip preserves medial and lateral anatomy.

Caching was a practical enabler: it avoided repeated DICOM decoding during training. The same preprocessing function was included in the inference path. Different cache generations remained paired with their own experiments.

Two pooling heads

The max head used a feature-wise maximum across valid windows. The attention head learned separate window weights for each finding, following attention MIL. Both used shared image features and predicted the same twelve targets.

attention[k, finding] = softmax(score(feature[k], finding))
pooled[finding]       = sum_k attention[k, finding] * feature[k]
logit[finding]        = classifier[finding](pooled[finding])

Finding-specific attention is an established method, not an algorithm we invented. Our question was whether it helped under our data and training budget.

Typical fitting used AdamW, cosine decay, fp16 AMP and batches of two studies. Most members trained for twelve epochs; E6 used sixteen. The final ensemble converted each member's predictions to percentile ranks and averaged them. These are cohort-relative scores, not calibrated clinical probabilities.

The independently trained line improved from 0.735 private for E01 to 0.837 for Ens4. Preprocessing, training duration, targets and model diversity all changed during that progression. We cannot assign each leaderboard gain to one component, even when its motivation was clear.

4. What our controlled tests actually showed

Pretraining helped; extra coverage needed more than a larger input

ImageNet initialization clearly helped this short ResNet18 protocol. Doubling training windows or spreading the same window count across two series did not help the matched baseline. S01 also required more decoding time despite keeping the encoder window budget fixed.

This does not mean more series are generally harmful. It means our particular selection, pooling and three-epoch schedule did not make good use of them.

Attention improved once, then failed to replicate

Figure 4. Attention minus max pooling on the fixed proxy endpoint. Intervals are paired known-group bootstraps for one fitted run; they do not estimate seed variance.

The first attention comparison gained +0.0123 macro AUC. In the second matched pair, its difference was -0.0020, with an interval crossing zero. Each pair used the same hardware internally, but the first pair ran locally and the second on Kaggle. The discrepancy therefore mixes seed and environment changes. It still prevents a reliable claim that attention was better in our setting based on the first result alone.

A later contextual package with slot/position embeddings and two Transformer layers also underperformed its six-slot attention control. A more complicated head did not automatically improve the reader.

5. A cheap follow-up: frozen DINOv2 features

We also tested a local frozen-feature pipeline. DINOv2 ViT-S/14 encoded 224-pixel windows once. Small heads then trained on CPU, using six plane/contrast slots, validity masks and optional within-series context and geometry.

Figure 5. Separate frozen-feature experiment on 863 proxy-validation studies. The geometry control was added post hoc, and all selected-epoch comparisons remain exploratory.

HeadProxy macro AUC
A: simple finding attention0.780321
B: context, slot and geometry inputs0.798874

The large A-to-B difference changed capacity, context and metadata together. The narrower B-versus-C diagnostic found only about +0.0014 attributable to the supplied geometry values. The ranking-loss arm gained about +0.0089 over B, while series dropout did not help this configuration.

The original feature extraction took about 7.4 minutes on a laptop GPU. The two follow-up F4 workers completed in 84 seconds on CPU, using zero Kaggle GPU hours. These were useful low-cost screening experiments, not evidence that the heads beat Ens4 or F0 on the hidden test. The panels have different recipes and references and should not be compared as one leaderboard.

6. The separate public-weights track

We reproduced the public stack from goodpjw2008 using nartaa's Raptor384 and Raptor224, heliosli's R6 and the upstream small reader. That F0 reproduction scored 0.958 public / 0.942 private.

We then explored public-model fine-tuning, compartment tiles, teacher-denoised labels, a student addition and inference-only blend variants.

None demonstrated a private gain over F0 at the displayed precision. Equal rounded AUCs do not imply identical predictions. The student looked acceptable under our development gates, yet its full-stack deployment regressed. Parent correlation and a small-set gain were not enough to justify that addition.

7. What failed and what we would change

  • Fine-tuning into a near-copy. Our low-rate, parent-anchored Raptor fine-tunes remained highly correlated with the parent. They did not establish useful incremental evidence for the full ensemble.
  • Numerical instability. Some EfficientNet and aggressive warm-start runs overflowed or collapsed. A broken run is not a fair architecture comparison.
  • Approximate compartment crops. Our fractional tiles did not provide the landmark-grounded, one-compartment-only view used by stronger specialist systems. Adding a zoom is not equivalent to anatomical supervision.
  • Weak validation transfer. Report-derived references were useful for controlled development, but they did not independently establish diagnostic performance or the value of a public-stack blend.
  • Late fusion experiments. Reweighting existing evidence was inexpensive, but it produced no demonstrated private gain in our final submissions.

For a future project we would prioritize a small, carefully evaluated image-first labeling pilot; one stable multiview baseline; one genuinely different anatomical specialist; and paired multi-seed confirmation before expanding an ensemble. We would measure the change to the complete proposed ensemble, rather than rely on a model's standalone score or decorrelation.

8. How this compares with the winning write-ups

The first-place author's currently published component uses image-reading agents, ROI/lesion annotations, a detector and ROI pooling, with training-only auxiliary targets. We did not build that spatial-supervision pipeline.

Third place combines image-derived targets, depth context, external OAI supervision and a much larger group-budgeted ensemble. Several ingredients overlap with ideas we explored, but their supervision and training programs were much more developed. Their write-ups do not isolate one change that would necessarily have closed our gap.

9. Reproducibility, limitations and acknowledgments

This is an empirical project write-up, not a new-architecture or clinical validation claim. Our early and frozen-feature panels use 863 validation studies; later N-series comparisons use 885. Bootstrap intervals condition on the fitted run. Multiple seeds, a fixed confirmation protocol and an independent reference remain necessary for stronger claims. The small expert set was used historically in development, so it cannot be presented as untouched throughout.

Code references, source hashes, aggregate tables and the detailed methods are available in our technical report. These figures contain aggregate results and original schematics only. AI assistance was used in implementation, analysis and writing; final scientific claims remain the responsibility of the project's human authors.
